records what each major branch tested, what the user heard, and what remains
useful. Active direction is summarized in
`docs/music/officewars/README.md`; this file exists so future work can reuse
earlier ideas without reviving rejected direction by accident.

The exhaustive pre-cleanup experiment log is preserved at
`archive/MUSIC_GUIDE_RULES_AND_EXPERIMENT_LOG_2026-08-13.md` in the source
workspace.

## 1. Early Identity And Energy

`All In on Company Time` established the fluid melodic direction that the user
liked. `Loaded Dice` also received a positive response, although its use of the
original melody meant its harmony was never isolated as the reason.

The early engine experiments established two broad energy families:

- Festival Hybrid for ordinary Casino play; and
- Redline for the dangerous state after a major loss.

The user liked the bounce and Casino character but found several other early
themes disconcerting because their melodies made large jumps and did not flow.
This created the durable preference for nearby melodic motion and prepared
destinations.

## 2. No Going Back Transition Series

### Original

`No Going Back` contained an ending the user liked, but the buildup removed
part of the natural approach and resumed with an octave-raised note. The
payoff felt attached rather than earned.

### Earned Release

This version restored the melodic approach into the payoff. The connection
improved, but the transition remained too short and abrupt.

### Long Fuse

Long Fuse began the transition earlier, added foreshadowing, narrowed the
stereo field, and opened the lead filter. It still retained too much low-end
and rhythmic weight for the payoff to reclaim.

### Contrast Reset

Contrast Reset withdrew wobble bass, kick weight, width, brightness, dryness,
and gain while preserving the melodic thread. The user found it acceptable,
then identified that its one-bar payoff ended before the impact had time to
stay.

### Hold The Rush

Hold the Rush extended the payoff from one bar to three bars while preserving
the buildup and first impact. The user liked it. The durable lesson is that the
high needs enough time to be enjoyed; the exact complete cue was not approved.

### Sugar Rush

Sugar Rush increased width, brightness, drum weight, offbeat bass movement,
lead body, and phrase pickups while preserving Hold the Rush's form. The user
called it a good direction and asked for a longer high.

## 3. Long Form, Crash, And Comedown

### Sugar Crash

The long form extended the Sugar Rush payoff and then dropped into crash,
regret, and afterimage. The first version became too quiet after the crash on
some playback systems.

### Audible Regret

This control increased post-crash midrange audibility while preserving the
form. The technical change worked, but the user later preferred removing the
hard crash rather than polishing it further.

### Smooth Comedown

Smooth Comedown gradually reduced drums, bass, brightness, width, and melodic
density. It avoided the hard crash but still felt wrong.

The boundary exposed several causes:

- an upward register jump;
- a harmony restart before the active progression completed;
- removal of the established swing; and
- several arrangement identities changing at once.

### Land The Rush

Land the Rush preserved the control's length and gain curve while completing
the harmony, beginning on the same pitch where the rush ended, preserving the
groove for one bar, and reducing layers in sequence.

This clarified the concept of a phrase-landed comedown, but the complete
long-form direction remained unresolved.

## 4. Relapse And Redline

### One More Bet

One More Bet treated the reduced-energy section as a false release and rebuilt
into a second, leaner drop. It preserved the first high as the larger peak and
used a dominant handoff into the loop.

### One More Bet - Redline

The Redline control increased the working tempo from 154 to 174 BPM, added
drum and bass pressure, and kept more movement through the lower sections.

### Redline Chase

Redline Chase added a gated midrange sequencer intended to communicate urgency
without becoming an irritating tapping layer.

The user found both Redline versions cohesive but roughly equal, stagnant, and
insufficiently urgent. The chase voice was too subtle to justify itself. The
buildup was acceptable, but the drop did not satisfy its promise.

The durable lesson is that speed and an urgency layer cannot solve unclear
macro form or weak payoff identity.

## 5. Build, Drop, And Compulsion Labs

### Build / Drop Lab

This isolated four bars of buildup and eight bars of payoff. The buildup
withdrew low end, width, brightness, dryness, and gain. The payoff restored
them and evolved every two bars.

A half-bar fakeout made the user feel that the music stopped. The rest remained
a pleasant tune rather than creating urgency or a strong physical response.
The version remains a technical contrast control only.

### Compulsion Engine

This version removed the fakeout and kept the kick-and-bass engine active
through Rush, Fixation, Panic, and Overload. It added a repeated bass hook,
syncopated pickups, faster answers, subdivisions, and increasing instability.

The user said it was on the right track and sounded good, but Overload was the
weakest section.

### Overload Rewrite

The rewrite introduced a counter-hook, changed bass rhythm, stronger impact,
dominant tension, and chromatic approaches. The user could hear the attempt to
connect Panic and Overload, but the handoff felt artificial.

### Panic Morph

Panic Morph foreshadowed the future hook, transferred the groove over a full
bar, and delayed the impact until after the transformation. It remained
technically continuous but still did not hit emotionally.

On a later listen, the user identified a stronger result inside that failed
whole-form verdict. They like the tune and movement through
`Rush -> Fixation -> Panic -> Panic Learns the Hook`. The sequence feels
causal, and Panic learning the hook is the most useful development idea.

The following `Panic Morph -> Release -> Overshoot` sequence is musically
acceptable, but its names do not describe the heard functions. Preserve those
notes only as unlabeled source material. Do not treat them as an approved
morph, release, or overshoot, and do not revive the whole cue as approved.

At this point, full-form patching stopped. The process moved to motif identity
because the cue lacked a sufficiently distinctive musical center.

## 6. Motif Identity Lab

The lab held tempo, harmony, groove, instruments, duration, output gain, and
final resolution constant.

### Bare Descent

The plain descending idea established readability.

### Mirrored Answer

The descent alternated with an ascending answer. The user found it good but
comparatively standard.

### Obsessive Fragment

Incomplete fragments revealed one more note on each return. The user loved its
identity but correctly warned that withheld completion created more places for
a longer piece to stall or feel gimmicky.

### Obsession Into Mirror

This combined the obsessive opening with one mirrored bridge. The user liked
it overall but heard an awkward handoff around four to seven seconds.

The delayed completion note was performing two jobs at once: finishing the old
phrase and beginning the new answer. That created a mechanical hinge.

### Prepared Mirror

Prepared Mirror allowed the descent to complete before giving the answer a
separate role, then returned to the established final completion.

The user said it was good and felt disappointed when it ended. This selected
Prepared Mirror as the motif foundation.

### Prepared Mirror: Second Pass

The second pass repeated Prepared Mirror's melody and harmony exactly while
gradually adding upper sheen and octave shadow. The user found the longer
version repetitive.

This is the clearest current evidence that arrangement-only growth cannot
sustain the foreground phrase. The next experiment needs a new phrase
function or destination.

The user called Production Pass 06 perfect. Corporate Contraband is therefore
