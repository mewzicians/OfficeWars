# Music Research Library

Last updated: 2026-08-18

## Purpose

This file stores the research basis behind `docs/MUSIC_GUIDE.md`. It keeps
source-specific lessons out of the main creative workflow so the guide can
remain coherent and project-agnostic.

Research informs experiments. It does not overrule listening evidence or turn
one producer's workflow into a universal law.

## Source Confidence

- **Directly reviewed text or transcript:** suitable for extracting supported
  concepts while still checking the author's context and claims.
- **Reviewed captions:** useful for broad lessons; ambiguous terminology is not
  treated as an exact quotation.
- **Practitioner discussion:** valuable experience and workflow evidence, not a
  universal standard.
- **Commercial tutorial:** useful when its practical advice is corroborated;
  product promotion and unsupported claims are discarded.
- **Listener evidence:** the user's repeated response is the strongest source
  for their taste and for whether our music communicates its intended effect.

## Durable Knowledge

### Emotion And Psychoacoustics

Reviewed material:

- UJAM on emotionally charged EDM;
- The Music Studio on harmony and emotional context; and
- RMCAD on psychoacoustics and production.

Durable lessons:

- Tempo, dynamics, register, density, articulation, harmony, timbre, space, and
  performance interact to shape emotion.
- Key or chord quality alone does not guarantee an emotional result.
- Active listening should identify what changed, when attention moved, and
  whether the intended emotional turn was actually heard.
- Production can alter perceived distance, intimacy, scale, urgency, and
  vulnerability.
- Emotional effectiveness needs listening feedback, variation, and revision,
  not only theoretical intention.

### Music Theory And Ear Training

Reviewed material:

- Avid's `Music Theory: Basics and Fundamental Concepts`, supplied as text;
- Pianote's beginner theory guide;
- Woochia's theory course;
- Rick Beato's theory masterclass; and
- the user's summary that theory, arrangement, and mixing are connected.

Durable lessons:

- Theory is a vocabulary for heard relationships.
- Pitch, duration, interval, rhythm, scale, key, harmony, voice leading,
  cadence, meter, subdivision, syncopation, and dynamics interact.
- Relative motion and contour often preserve identity more strongly than
  absolute pitch.
- Voice leading and bass movement can make harmony feel inevitable or
  disrupted.
- Ear-first work remains essential: imagine, sing, play, identify, then revise
  intentionally.

### Motif, Melody, And Form

Reviewed material:

- Ethan Sherman on motivic development;
- Learn Jazz Standards episode 108;
- Ronald Caltabiano's motive-development handout;
- Art of Composing on simple musical form;
- Shed the Music on melodic development and EDM;
- Jason Yu's Undertale analysis;
- Notes and Dreams on Undertale composition; and
- supplied videos discussing Undertale motifs and development.

Durable lessons:

- A motif can be rhythmic, melodic, harmonic, textural, or gestural.
- Recognition depends on a small number of invariants such as rhythm, contour,
  interval profile, accent, or destination.
- Repetition creates recognition; development changes context, function, or
  destination.
- Fragmentation, sequence, inversion, extension, compression, augmentation,
  diminution, reharmonization, and reorchestration have different effects.
- Reorchestration can connect scenes, but timbre alone may not provide enough
  horizontal development for a foreground phrase.
- Simple material can support a large emotional world when it is transformed
  with purpose.
- A phrase needs a role in the form, not merely a place on the timeline.

### High-Energy Thematic Transformation

Directly reviewed material:

- `Pokémon Legends: Z-A - Jacinthe Battle Music (HQ)`;
- Toby Fox's `Hopes And Dreams`; and
- `Pokémon B2/W2 - Champion Iris Battle Music (HQ)`.

The tracks were reviewed as references for craft qualities, not as templates.
Temporary direct-audio inspection corroborated broad structural observations:
the Jacinthe and Iris uploads contain strongly recurring forms of roughly
`89.9` and `92.5` seconds, while `Hopes And Dreams` uses recurring phrase
modules inside a longer approximately three-minute escalation. Automated
tempo, onset, recurrence, dynamics, and spectral measurements are supporting
evidence, not a substitute for listening or score analysis.

Durable lessons:

- A transformed cue needs both recognizable ancestry and a scene identity that
  works without quotation.
- Callbacks become development when they change function. A fragment can drive
  rhythm, a cadence can prepare an arrival, a harmonic memory can destabilize
  a new theme, and a fuller statement can become payoff.
- High energy can come from subdivision, phrase turnover, bass and drum motion,
  harmonic rhythm, articulation, register, brightness, role rotation, and
  transition compression even when the measured beat is moderate.
- Stable high intensity remains clearer when musical roles, register, color,
  or phrase destination change more often than the underlying identity.
- Emotional culmination is stronger when earlier contrast makes the final
  brightness, width, density, or thematic completion feel reclaimed.
- A compact repeating form can sustain confrontation when internal sections
  have distinct functions and the seam participates in the momentum.
- Callback quantity is not a success metric. Too many equal quotations weaken
  hierarchy and can turn transformation into a medley.
- Reference-specific instrumentation, harmony, melody, and production are not
  universal energy rules. Transfer the causal relationship, then rebuild it
  from the new work's motif, scene, palette, and dramatic purpose.

### Boss Character, Humor, And Status Transformation

Directly reviewed material:

- Shady Cicada's final-boss composition tutorial and completed example;
- Tarro57's silly-battle composition tutorial;
- 8-bit Music Theory's analysis of motivic development in `Gwyn, Lord of
  Cinder`; and
- Snazzy Spider's unexpected-NPC boss battle cue.

Durable lessons:

- Boss identity does not require erasing a character's ordinary, comic, or
  fragile identity. The existing identity can be promoted through greater
  phrase authority, bass weight, register, harmonic consequence, ensemble
  scale, and cadence.
- Expectation includes narrative role and genre convention, not only musical
  setup. A final boss can surprise by becoming quieter and lonelier, while an
  unimportant NPC can surprise when familiar eccentric material gains genuine
  weight and competence.
- Humor can live in articulation, instrumentation, register, and rhythmic
  behavior while harmony and form remain coherent. A funny battle cue still
  benefits from leading tones, voice leading, sectional contrast, withheld
  completion, and an intentional loop seam.
- Sparse or incomplete early statements create developmental reserve. Later
  variants can fill missing beats, extend an answer, combine motifs, or occupy
  register that was intentionally withheld.
- Tiny motifs remain useful when their function changes. Inversion,
  augmentation, phrase truncation, call and response, changed accompaniment,
  and combination at the climax can produce long-form growth without replacing
  the identity.
- A comic reveal becomes credible only when the music eventually commits to
  stakes. Constant winking can preserve the joke while preventing the listener
  from believing the character is dangerous.
- Meter changes, chromatic motor figures, jumpy bass-and-chord interlocks, and
  temporary subtraction can increase pressure without requiring continuous
  maximum density.

The unexpected-NPC cue's direct audio analysis found an approximately
`116.4-second` form. Its first roughly `30.6` seconds establish identity; the
next approximately `20.2` seconds nearly double average signal energy and
increase brightness; a low-register withdrawal occupies much of
approximately `71.2-89.7`; and the strongest statement arrives around
`89.7-111.6`. Broad chroma similarity remains high across early and later
sections, supporting tonal ancestry without proving note-for-note melodic
identity.

These observations are reference evidence, not mandatory section lengths or
an orchestration template.

### EDM Buildups, Drops, And Flow

Reviewed material:

- UJAM on hard-hitting EDM drops;
- iZotope on drop layering and mix focus;
- Icon Collective on drop production;
- Boombox on buildups and transitions;
- PhraseBend on EDM melody, chords, and flow;
- EDMProd on buildups; and
- Soundverse on EDM melody.

Durable lessons:

- Impact is created by contrast and expectation, not only absolute loudness.
- A buildup may withdraw low end, width, brightness, density, dryness, or
  rhythmic weight so the payoff can reclaim them.
- Longer buildups make larger promises.
- Low-frequency restoration, rhythmic arrival, harmonic resolution, and a
  recognizable hook can land together for physical impact.
- Silence or a breath works only when the phrase and groove continue to point
  through it.
- The payoff should develop after impact rather than remain one static block.
- Layering is useful when every layer has a perceptual role and the focal idea
  stays legible.
- More source density or tempo cannot repair a weak macro form.

Commercial or unsupported claims about neurological responses, mandatory bar
counts, or one correct drop formula are not retained.

### Sound Design And Evolving Timbre

Reviewed material:

- the user-supplied transcript of
  `How to Make Evolving Sounds | OVO Series Pt 3`.

Durable lessons:

- Harmony and voicing can be checked on a clear instrument before sound
  substitution.
- Envelope, filter, harmonic-content, pitch, pan, delay, and reverb movement
  can make a stable role evolve.
- Slow modulation can introduce upper harmonics or reverse-swell character.
- Detuning, pitch LFO, portamento, and auto-pan often work best below obvious
  perception unless instability is the intended feature.
- Establishing the groove before detailed sub-bass movement can produce a
  clearer low-end relationship.
- Simple root-based sub-bass is a reliable starting point, not a limitation.
- Occasional drum variants become meaningful after the groove is established.
- Playback systems may reveal brightness or balance differences that one set
  of monitors hides.

Plugin promotion, genre prescriptions, and uncertain presenter explanations
are not retained as general truth.

### Arrangement, Production, And Mixing

Reviewed material:

- Mastering.com's long-form mixing course;
- RMCAD production guidance;
- iZotope and Icon Collective material; and
- the supplied Berklee summary covering theory, arrangement, and mixing.

Durable lessons:

- Horizontal arrangement controls form over time; vertical arrangement
  controls the simultaneous hierarchy.
- Sound selection and arrangement solve many conflicts before EQ or
  compression.
- Static fader and pan balance should reveal the focal point before detailed
  processing.
- Compression changes envelope and groove as well as dynamic range.
- Saturation adds harmonics and audibility while consuming headroom.
- Reverb and delay create space and continuity but can mask rhythm and
  accumulate energy.
- Loudness-matched comparison is necessary when judging arrangement or tone.
- Translation checks should include low volume, mono, headphones, and multiple
  speaker systems.

### Game Music, Loops, And Adaptive Structure

Reviewed material:

- `A Guide to Making Video Game Music` by Zectro;
- `How to Make a Video Game Soundtrack (from scratch)` by Jaies;
- Composer Code's game-music guidance;
- Musicians Institute on game composition;
- the user-supplied VI-Control practitioner discussion;
- `What are "Game Loops"? Explained with Music!`; and
- `Quick Game Audio Looping Tutorial`.

Durable lessons:

- Gameplay function and listening duration shape musical density and fatigue.
- Loop endings should be composed against their beginnings.
- Layers can repeat on different time scales to create stability and change.
- Horizontal transitions, vertical layers, and stingers solve different
  interactive needs.
- State mappings need musically stable thresholds and intentional sync points.
- Adaptive music should preserve identity across states.
- Technical implementation, naming, state cleanup, and transition testing are
  part of the composition.

### Web Audio Platform Behaviour

Source: first-party measurement in this project, not a reviewed source.
Confirmed twice independently and then by the user's ear.

Durable lessons:

- A Chromium `DynamicsCompressorNode` reports roughly **-16.5 dB of gain
  reduction before it has received a single sample of audio**, then releases
  upward. Measured at threshold -11, knee 5, ratio 11, attack 0.002,
  release 0.13: above -6 dB after 0.37 s, -3 dB after 0.60 s, -1 dB after
  0.95 s, -0.5 dB after 1.18 s, settling at a permanent -0.12 dB. Music that
  begins within about a second of building the graph is played through that
  upward sweep, which is audible as static or a pump.
- **That length is not a constant. It is a property of the node's own
  settings, so measure it per configuration rather than copying a known-good
  number.** Three configurations measured in one context: threshold -11 / ratio
  11 and threshold -10 / ratio 12 both start near -16.5 dB and clear -0.5 dB at
  1.181 s, while threshold -8 / knee 4 / ratio 20 starts at **-20.72 dB** and
  clears at 1.251 s, settling at -0.152 dB rather than -0.122 dB. A deeper
  threshold with a higher ratio buys a deeper start and a longer wait. Reusing
  the shallower page's 1.25 s on the deeper node would have left part of a
  20.7 dB sweep inside the music while every static check still passed.
- **`compressor.reduction` reads exactly 0 before the node has processed a
  sample**, not its initial deep value. So scanning forward for the first
  reading above a threshold matches that pre-render zero and reports every
  level cleared at 0.000 s — the opposite of the truth, and it looks like a
  clean result rather than an error. Find engagement first, by scanning for the
  first reading past a shallow floor, then measure crossings from there. Same
  class as `outputLatency` reading 0 on a context that has never rendered:
  values sampled before the render thread starts are absent, not zero.
- Give such a measurement a **control**: measure a configuration whose crossings
  are already recorded and assert they reproduce before believing the unmeasured
  ones in the same run. Two separate defects were caught this way — the
  pre-render zero above, and a rounding rule that failed to regenerate the
  constant already in use — and in both cases the raw crossings were correct
  while the reported answer was not.
- The remedy is to let silence flow through the graph before the first note.
  Time the wait in **rendered** time, not wall-clock time, because the detector
  state only appears once the render thread begins, roughly 120 ms after the
  graph is built in the measured case.
- A pre-roll over about a second becomes a **user-interface** problem, not only
  an audio one. A transport that is genuinely running but silent reads as a hung
  control, so it needs its own label. Repaint that label from an existing
  interval: the wait ends on the audio clock and dispatches no event, so a label
  set once at start will still be showing the waiting state after the music has
  begun. Gate it on the wait being longer than the ordinary scheduling lead, or
  it flashes on every start that is not waiting for anything.
- The defect appears **only on the first play after a page load**. A graph built
  once per load and never rebuilt means looping, seeking, and stop-then-restart
  all inherit a settled node, so those three cannot reproduce it. Distinguish
  "first play" from "first play after a reload" when testing.
- A post-compressor analyser cannot see this. Peak and RMS readings report what
  the compressor already did, so a clean meter is not evidence of a clean
  signal. Read `compressor.reduction`, or tap the graph ahead of the node.
- Fan-out is the non-invasive way to tap a live graph: connecting an extra
  analyser leaves the original destination receiving exactly what it received
  before, so the measurement cannot create the artifact it is measuring.
- Judge such a repair by how much gain change remains **while music plays**,
  against roughly 1 dB as a just-noticeable slow level change, rather than by
  how close the reduction reading is to zero.
- Chromium's default `latencyHint` is `interactive`, which requests the
  **smallest** render buffer the device will give. Measured `baseLatency`:
  `interactive`, `balanced`, and the default all give **10 ms**; `'playback'`
  gives **20 ms**; an explicit `0.2` gives 170 ms. A listening page has no
  interactive latency requirement, so the smallest buffer is the wrong default
  for it: buffer size is what absorbs a late render, and the smallest buffer
  maximizes exposure to rare one-off crackle.
- Do not simply request the largest buffer. A scheduler that looks ahead by a
  fixed horizon must schedule at least one buffer in advance, so a 170 ms buffer
  under a 180 ms lookahead leaves almost no margin and converts a mitigation
  into systematic lateness.
- `outputLatency` reads **0 on a context that has never rendered**, so it cannot
  be used to detect whether a real output device exists until audio is playing.
  `baseLatency` is available immediately.
- A rare, non-repeatable artifact needs a **recurrence test**, not a threshold.
  On a deterministic schedule the same musical position produces the same
  waveform on every loop, so folding captured events into loop position and
  intersecting across loops separates what is composed in from what the run time
  inserted. A single-pass threshold count cannot make that distinction.
- Set the capture threshold **inside** the music's own transient distribution and
  assert on a separate, higher threshold. A capture threshold above every real
  transient logs nothing, and a recurrence test over two empty sets passes while
  proving nothing.
- Exclude the startup interval when comparing `audioContext.currentTime` against
  a wall clock. Before the render thread begins, audio time is stationary while
  wall clock advances, which is indistinguishable from a large dropout and is
  not one.
- Detect a dropout as a **sustained** step in cumulative audio-behind-wall
  offset, never as a single-interval difference. A sampler on the main thread
  competes with the page's own paint work, so one late callback makes a `20 ms`
  interval look `12 ms` short and then recovers. Audio that was never rendered
  does not come back, so a real gap stays. Requiring the offset to remain
  elevated for `250 ms` separates them.
- Net drift is the cross-check that catches this class of false positive. Five
  reported `12 ms` shortfalls on a run whose net drift was `+6.7 ms` cannot all
  be real, because real gaps accumulate into permanent lag. Compare the sum of
  claimed gaps against the net drift before believing either.
- An `AudioWorkletNode` is the right probe for sample-level inspection because it
  runs on the render thread. A `ScriptProcessorNode` runs its callback on the
  main thread inside the render path, so using one to hunt dropouts adds
  main-thread work to the chain being measured and manufactures the failure.
- A worklet module cannot be loaded from a blob URL on a `file://` origin.
  Serving the page over a local HTTP server for the measurement changes the
  document's origin, not its audio graph, schedule, or render thread.

### References And Originality

Lessons synthesized across the reviewed sources:

- Analyze qualities rather than copying expression.
- Compare several references when possible.
- Transcribe only enough to understand a relationship.
- Rebuild the idea from a new brief, motif, harmony, rhythm, palette, and
  performance.
- Track samples, performers, licenses, and source material before delivery.

## Current Knowledge Gaps

The guide is now broad enough to support work beyond games, but our research is
still much deeper in instrumental electronic music than in several other
areas. Useful future research would include:

- lyric writing, storytelling, rhyme, and prosody;
- vocal production, comping, tuning philosophy, and singer health;
- orchestration and idiomatic writing for live instruments;
- arranging for bands, ensembles, and acoustic spaces;
- microphone technique, phase, room treatment, and recording workflow;
- mastering, loudness standards, metadata, and delivery by platform;
- genre history and cultural context;
- hearing safety and long-session production practice;
- accessibility and music perception across different hearing profiles; and
- adaptive-audio middleware and production pipelines beyond the current Web
  Audio prototype.

These gaps do not prevent experimentation. They tell me where I should be more
careful about certainty and where additional sources could improve future
work.
